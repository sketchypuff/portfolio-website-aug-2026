Photoshop Express offered 3 preset masks. A new masking engine could detect
15–20 more from the image itself, and the existing masking flow couldn’t hold
them without discoverability and usability problems. It needed rethinking.

Two months, with a design manager, a product manager, and two engineers.

## Impact

The new masks widened what people could do with an image and drove
significant free-to-premium conversions.

- **5%** increase in free users converting to premium
- **40%** throughput of selecting objects in the image
- **27%** throughput of selecting skin in the image

## Photoshop Express

Photoshop Express is a mobile-only photo and video editing app that helps
social media enthusiasts express their creativity without complex workflows.

<Figure src="what-is-psx.png" alt="Overview of the Photoshop Express app" />

## Before

Users masked areas of an image with preset mask pills. Three of them. So smol,
right? I know.

<Figure src="mask-pills.png" alt="The original strip of three preset mask pills" />

## The problem

Adding more masks to the strip meant excessive scrolling. Masks toward the end
of the strip suffered for it, because, let’s face it, who’s going to scroll
that much?

<Figure src="long-scroll.png" alt="A long horizontal strip of 20+ mask pills" />

## Market audit

Surprisingly, no other app offered as many masks. Of the 10 studied, only 2
offered masking at all.

<Figure src="market-audit.png" alt="Audit of masking features across ten editing apps" />

## Iteration 1: the strip

I started with what we had: the same strip, holding 20+ masks, grouped into
categories with their contents nested inside.

- The strip overlaps key areas of the image, making refining and brushing a
  mask difficult.
- Small previews make it hard to decide between masks.
- Selecting several masks gets tricky in a small, cramped space.

<Figure src="exp1-strip.png" alt="Mask categories nested inside the existing strip" />

## Iteration 2: e-commerce filters

Aren’t masks just filters on an image? E-commerce apps have well-worn patterns
for filtering, so I borrowed them.

<Figure src="ecommerce-inspiration.png" alt="Filtering patterns from e-commerce apps" />

They organized the masks well, but without previews users had to guess which
part of the image a label like “Object 1” referred to.

- Better organization of masks.
- Users select masks blindly, by trial and error.

<Figure src="exp2-ecommerce.png" alt="Masks selected through an e-commerce style filter sheet" />

## Iteration 3: previews

Showing a preview of each mask let users see exactly which area of the image
it covers.

- Bigger previews make selection accurate.
- Laying out every category leads to a long vertical scroll that hampers
  search and discovery.

<Figure src="exp3-bottom-sheet.png" alt="A bottom sheet of masks with image previews" />

## Iteration 4: navigation

Category pills above the list. Tapping one scrolls the list to the first mask
in that category.

- Browsing gets faster.
- The bottom sheet takes up about half the screen, shrinking the image too much.

<Figure src="exp4-navigation.png" alt="Category pills above the mask preview list" />

## Final design

Two levels of navigation was already the pattern for most tools in the editor,
and reusing it fixed the pitfalls of the earlier iterations.

- Category navigation stays, so browsing is still fast.
- A shorter bottom sheet leaves a larger image preview.
- An existing pattern makes it easier to learn.

<Figure src="final-design.png" alt="Final masking design with two levels of navigation" />

## Custom masks

Add Selection lets users brush a mask of their own. Custom masks are saved in
their own category.

<Figure src="custom-selection.png" alt="Brushing a custom mask with Add Selection" />

## Refining a mask

With a mask selected, Add Selection becomes Refine Selection, letting users add
or subtract areas to refine it.

<Figure src="refine-selection.png" alt="Refining a preset mask with Refine Selection" />

## Takeaways

This project was packed with work, exploration, and discussion with
stakeholders. Boiled down:

**Looking outside the box.** Reducing the requirement to its core user need and
drawing metaphors let me take inspiration from apps outside the domain, which
brought a fresh perspective even though that concept was rejected.

**The challenges of scale.** I learned how scale changes interaction in so
little screen space, and how patterns can guide what users do.
