**Typical case study structures read like clinical reports.** They follow a rigid
format: introduction, problem statement, process, solution, impact. While
comprehensive, this approach often puts readers to sleep before they reach the
most interesting parts of your work.

_So how do we make it less boring?_

Hi folks, I’m Yash, a product designer working at Adobe. I’ll credit the
internship that landed this role to telling a story about my work effectively,
aiming to help the recruiter/interviewer/reader believe these 3 things strongly
and as early as possible, when they skim my portfolio:

1. I believe in the problem you’re claiming to solve, and that its worthy of solving.
2. I believe in the solutions you’ve ideated, and the logic on which you’ve chosen a solution amongst the ideations.
3. I understood all that under ~90s of skimming, enough to peak my interest to call you for an interview.

**Here are a few guidelines to tell seamless stories and land interviews. Use
them as guidelines and not rigid rules.**

## Reports vs. stories

Here’s what reports look like:

1. Fact 1
2. Fact 2
3. Fact 3

Here’s what stories look like:

1. This is the situation
2. Then this happens
3. Because of which, this happens
4. Because of which, this happens
5. Finally, this happens, and happily ever after

Notice how a list of facts act like individual chunks of information and make
you feel just as bored as when you read a college textbook? Trying to force you
to remember things, but you don’t feel like it?

And notice how story chunks flow into each other seamlessly, where every chunk
is related and comes as a consequence of the previous one, and the foundation
to the next one?

Which kind are you most likely to remember? That’s the kind a recruiter will
too. That’s what you want.

## Grab a friend. Tell them a story.

Yep, its that straightforward.

**Pick a friend, preferably someone who’s not a designer, and talk to them about
your project under 1 minute.** If they get it, great. If they don’t, probe for
why, until you have a one minute elevator pitch which they understand.

Once you find that one minute story, use that to guide your case study. Voila!
You’ve magically created a case study skeleton that doesn’t sound like it was
written by a robot trying to impress other robots. It’s authentic, jargon-free,
and might actually contain traces of your personality.

This urgency induced by the 1 minute time limit, and conversational nature of
the narration is actually your secret weapon for clarity. You’ll notice how you
skim over insignificant details and get to the juicy ones.

## The ‘so what’ test

**Ask ‘so what?’ to every section in your case study.**

1. You conducted interviews? So what? You learnt that the user faced X-Y-Z problem.
2. You brainstormed? So what? You ideated solutions on the lines of X-Y-Z goals.
3. You explore 100 different solutions? So what? You found out X-Y-Z solutions fulfilled your goals better than the rest.
4. You conducted usability tests? So what? You found proof X-Y-Z goals were fulfilled/not fulfilled.

Be honest to yourself, if the answers to ‘so what?’ do not significantly
contribute to communicating the problem, the solution, or the decisions taken in
between, it has no place in your case study.

## Descriptive titles for seamless skimming

Let’s recall the ‘narrating to a friend’ exercise. You won’t have naturally said
‘Research’, ‘Ideation’ and ‘Testing’, before describing that part, right?

You’d just flow into what matters. Emulate the same flow in the case study.

**Club the narrative and ‘so what’ exercise to write descriptive titles in your
case study.** Skip boring titles like ‘Research’, ‘Ideation’, and ‘Testing’.

1. Instead of ‘Interviews’, describe what you learnt out of them. A better title to that section would simply be: Users were not finding that damn button in the checkout flow.
2. Instead of ‘Brainstorming’, say: I thought of ideas mainly on the lines of reducing time on task.
3. Instead of ‘Concept 1’, say: Making that button more prominent.

## Help the reader ‘empathize’. For real this time.

People give more sh*ts when they’re able to relate to someone. In a case study,
this creates an emotional connection that keeps stakeholders interested and
believe in what you’re saying.

**Show problems as real situations users might face in everyday life.** For
example, instead of saying ‘users had difficulty with navigation’, describe how
‘[Enter persona] got frustrated trying to find the checkout button while rushing
to complete her purchase.’

**Help the readers ‘see’ and ‘imagine’ that problem occurring in real life.** If
they believe it, they’ll believe you.

## Tweet it!

Most case studies are super verbose! They’re just like: Here’s a generic title →
Blah blah long explanation nobody will read → Check out this pic → Repeat
forever.

**Try this instead: Pretend your case study is a Twitter thread.**

Come up with a catchy title that makes people wanna click, then keep your
explanations super short, like tweet-sized short (under 280 characters, or 2
sentences.). This way, you’ll get straight to the point without boring everyone
with unnecessary details.

**PS: You might wanna use Twitter to prototype your thread. If you’re feeling
confident, post it too!**

## POV: You’re a magazine editor

I learnt this from one of my seniors during my time at design uni. Be a magazine
editor for the time being.

**Grab all pieces of text and arrange them in order. Then grab all images and
arrange them in order separately. If both these lists (somewhat) communicate a
story, and the same story, you have a skim-able case study.**

Place a relevant, well crafted image after text sections to highlight your main
points. This shows important parts of your story visually while giving the
reader a break from the chunks of text.

This is why comics, graphic novels and magazines are easier to read than
conventional books, and why people finish them faster. When your images tell a
clear story, they make complex ideas easier to understand than words alone.

## BLUF it

Learnt this from some YT video of a McKinsey dude talking about business
presentations. BLUF means Bottom Line, Up Front.

**That means before elaborating on your lengthy case study, within a section,
explain in a broader perspective, the problem, the solution, and the impact (any
metrics moved).**

Help get the readers on the same page, and let them know what to expect from
the case study. It helps quell any stray thoughts that makes them ‘imagine’
something different and get dissatisfied as they go through the case study.
